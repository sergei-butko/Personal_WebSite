Olfactory memory has a reputation for permanence. Proust gets cited, someone
mentions the amygdala, and the conversation proceeds as though the nose keeps
receipts.

It does not. Smell memory is unusually *durable* and unusually *imprecise* at the
same time, and the hobby has built a great deal of confident argument on top of
that combination.

## What the nose actually stores

You do not store a spectrum. You store a label, an emotional valence, and a
handful of salient features — and you reconstruct the rest on recall, from
expectation.

This is why:

- People reliably recognise a scent they cannot name.
- People reliably fail to notice a gradual change in a scent they wear daily.
- Blind, the same people often prefer the batch they insist is inferior.

The third one is the uncomfortable one, and it reproduces every time somebody
runs it properly.

> The bottle you remember is a composite: the actual perfume, the year you wore
> it, and every time you have described it since.

## The retelling problem

Each recall rewrites the memory. Describe a fragrance as "smokier back then" ten
times, and the smokiness becomes part of what you retrieve. The description is
not reporting the memory any more; it is editing it.

Reformulation discourse is a machine for doing this at scale. A thread forms, a
consensus forms, and everybody's memory drifts toward the consensus — including
the memories of people who have not smelled the old version in a decade.

## What survives this

Reformulation is real. Materials get restricted, suppliers change, costs get cut,
and the results are measurable. What does not survive is *uncorroborated personal
memory* as evidence for it.

What does:

- Side-by-side comparison, same session, same skin.
- Blind, or at minimum code-blind — someone else hands you the strips.
- Ingredient lists, which are regulatory and dated.
- A base-note difference, which oxidation cannot easily produce.

<Note>
  If you take one thing from this: run the comparison blind. Not because you are
  suggestible in some unusual way, but because everyone is, and the effect is
  large enough to swamp the difference you are trying to measure.
</Note>

## The version I am least sure about

I have a memory of a 1990s Guerlain that I am fairly certain is partly invented —
assembled from a decant, a photograph, and a description I read before I ever
smelled it. I cannot separate the layers now. That is the honest position for
most vintage claims, mine included.
