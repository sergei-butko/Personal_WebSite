There is a particular kind of thread that appears under every popular masculine
release once it passes its tenth birthday. Someone buys a fresh bottle, finds it
thinner than the one they remember, and announces a reformulation. Someone else
replies that noses change. Neither side produces a batch code.

I have two bottles. This is what I can actually say about them, and — more
importantly — what I cannot.

## What I am comparing

<Note>
  Two bottles is a sample size of two. Storage history dominates almost every
  variable below, and I know the provenance of exactly one of them. Read this as
  a method, not a verdict.
</Note>

| | Older bottle | Newer bottle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Batch code | `4501` | `2334` |
| Filled | early 2014 | mid 2023 |
| Fill level | ~70% | sealed until March |
| Stored | dark cupboard, stable | shop shelf, unknown |

The older bottle has been opened for a decade, which matters more than the
decade itself. Headspace oxidation does most of its work in the first year after
a bottle drops below half.

## The opening

The difference people report is real and it is in the first ninety seconds. The
older bottle has a wetter, greener citrus — grapefruit peel with something
slightly bitter under it. The newer one reads brighter and more transparent,
closer to a lemon-mint accord than a grapefruit one.

That is exactly the signature you would expect from **either** a reformulation
**or** ten years of oxidation. Citrus top notes are the first thing to go. This
is why opening-note comparisons are close to worthless as evidence, and why
almost every reformulation thread is built on them.

## The part that is harder to explain away

The drydown is where I start to believe something moved.

- The older bottle keeps a dry, faintly smoky incense note at the four-hour mark.
- The newer one replaces it with something cleaner and woodier — more ambroxan,
  less labdanum.
- Projection is comparable. Longevity on skin is comparable.

Oxidation does not usually *add* a note. When a base smells different in a
direction the older bottle never went, reformulation becomes the simpler
explanation.

> The base is the reformulation tell. Tops drift on their own; bases only move
> when someone moves them.

## Reading the codes

Chanel's four-character codes are not a date, they are a production reference —
but the first two digits track the year closely enough to place a bottle within
about twelve months.

```text
4501
││└┴─ production sequence within the period
│└─── period marker
└──── year reference (2014 → 4)
```

I go through this properly in [reading batch codes](/en/blog/reading-batch-codes/),
including the houses whose codes are genuinely useless.

## What I would need to be sure

A sealed 2014 bottle. Failing that, a decant from one stored in the dark since
purchase, with a code I can read myself. Until one of those turns up, the honest
answer is: the base changed, probably; the top note evidence proves nothing; and
anyone claiming certainty from a shelf bottle is guessing with extra steps.
